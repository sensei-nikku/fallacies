Logical Fallacies

A fallacy is a mistake in reasoning that makes an argument look stronger than it really is. Learn to spot them.

Ad Hominem

1
Attacking the person making the argument instead of the argument itself.
"You think we should recycle more? You drove a gas-guzzler for ten years."
"Why should we listen to her idea? She didn't even finish high school."
Why it matters: Someone's personal history doesn't make their argument right or wrong. This one shows up constantly in workplace disagreements and online arguments.

Straw Man

2
Misrepresenting what someone said so it's easier to argue against — you're fighting a "straw man" version of their point, not the real one.
Person A: "I think we should have a budget for eating out." Person B: "So you want to control every dollar I spend?"
"You want later school start times? So you think kids should just sleep all day?"
Why it matters: This kills productive conversations. In relationships and at work, learning to respond to what someone actually said changes everything.

False Dilemma

3
Presenting only two options when there are actually more. Forces you into a corner that doesn't exist.
"You're either with us or against us."
"You can either work overtime or lose your job."
Why it matters: People use this to pressure you into decisions. Recognizing it gives you the power to say, "Actually, there's a third option."

Appeal to Authority

4
Claiming something is true because an "expert" said so — especially when that expert has no expertise in the topic.
"My dentist says this is the best investment strategy."
"A famous athlete wears these shoes, so they must be the best."
Why it matters: Celebrity endorsements, influencer culture, and even well-meaning advice from friends rely on this. The question is always: are they actually an expert in this specific thing?

Bandwagon (Appeal to Popularity)

5
Arguing that something is true or good because lots of people believe it or do it.
"Millions of people use this app — it has to be good."
"Everybody cheats on their taxes a little. It's no big deal."
Why it matters: Popularity doesn't equal quality or correctness. This is the engine behind trends, scams, and peer pressure.

Red Herring

6
Introducing an unrelated topic to distract from the original argument.
"We were talking about your missing assignments, but sure, let's talk about how the bus was late last week."
"Why are you worried about my spending? The economy is bad for everyone right now."
Why it matters: People (including us) use this to dodge accountability. Spotting it keeps conversations on track.

Slippery Slope

7
Claiming that one small step will inevitably lead to a chain of extreme consequences — without evidence for the chain.
"If we let students use calculators, soon they won't be able to add 2+2."
"If I let you stay out past midnight once, next thing I know you'll be out all night every night."
Why it matters: This shows up in parenting, politics, and policy debates constantly. Sometimes chains of consequences are real — the fallacy is assuming they're inevitable without evidence.

Circular Reasoning

8
Using your conclusion as your evidence. The "proof" just restates the claim in different words.
"This is the best restaurant in town because no other restaurant is as good."
"I'm trustworthy because I'm telling you I'm honest."
Why it matters: This sounds convincing on the surface because the logic "feels" complete. It's not. Nothing was actually proven.

Tu Quoque (Whataboutism)

9
Deflecting criticism by pointing out that the other person does (or did) the same thing. Latin for "you too."
"You can't tell me to stop smoking — you used to smoke!"
"Don't lecture me about being late — you were late last Tuesday."
Why it matters: Someone being a hypocrite doesn't make their point wrong. This is the #1 way arguments go sideways in relationships.

Hasty Generalization

10
Drawing a broad conclusion from too few examples.
"I had a bad experience at that store once. That whole chain is terrible."
"My neighbor from [city] was rude, so people from there must all be rude."
Why it matters: This is the root of stereotyping and snap judgments. One experience ≠ a universal truth.

Post Hoc (False Cause)

11
"After this, therefore because of this." Assuming that because B happened after A, A must have caused B.
"I started wearing my lucky socks and then we won three games in a row."
"I ate at that new restaurant and got sick the next day — their food must be bad."
Why it matters: Superstitions, health misinformation, and conspiracy theories all lean on this. Correlation isn't causation.

Appeal to Emotion

12
Using feelings (fear, pity, anger, flattery) instead of logic to win an argument.
"If you really loved me, you wouldn't question my decisions."
"Think about the children!" (when the topic has nothing to do with children)
Why it matters: Emotions are valid, but they aren't evidence. Advertisers, manipulative people, and clickbait all exploit this.

Loaded Question

13
Asking a question that has an assumption baked in, so any answer seems like an admission.
"Have you stopped being lazy yet?"
"Why do you always make bad decisions?"
Why it matters: These questions are traps. The correct response is to challenge the assumption, not answer the question as asked.

Equivocation

14
Switching the meaning of a word mid-argument to make the logic seem to work.
"I have the right to say what I want. So what I said must be right."
"The sign says 'fine for parking here.' So it must be fine to park here."
Why it matters: This one is sneaky. People (and ads) slide between meanings of the same word to trick you.

No True Scotsman

15
When someone is proven wrong, they change the definition to exclude the counterexample. Moving the goalposts.
"No good parent would let their kid have a phone." "My neighbor is a great parent and her kid has a phone." "Well, no truly good parent would."
"Real men don't cry." (Redefining "real" to exclude anyone who doesn't fit.)
Why it matters: This makes a claim impossible to disprove by constantly changing what counts. It shuts down conversation.
